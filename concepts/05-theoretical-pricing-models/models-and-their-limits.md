# Models and their limits

**One-line definition:** a model is a simplified, manageable picture of the real world; it is only as good as its assumptions and the numbers fed into it.

## Intuition
A model aeroplane flies in a wind tunnel, not in a storm. A map is useful because it leaves things out, but it is not the territory. An option pricing model is a *formula* that turns a few inputs into a value. It is someone's idea of how an option *ought* to be valued under certain conditions.

Two things can go wrong:
1. **The model itself is wrong or too simple.** The real world breaks one of its assumptions.
2. **The inputs are wrong.** "Garbage in, garbage out."

So there is no guarantee that a model's value is accurate, or that it resembles the price in the market.

## The candle analogy (the book's)
A new trader without a model is like someone groping around in a dark room. A trader with a model carries a **candle**:
- He can make out the general layout of the room.
- He cannot see every detail, and the flicker distorts some of what he sees.
- Still, he is far better off than with no light at all.

The real danger comes as the trader gets more confident and trades bigger. A misreading of the dim, flickering light is then **magnified**, and a small mistake can become a financial disaster.

## What to do about it
- Use the model, but know what it can and cannot do.
- Most successful option traders use some kind of pricing model, because of what it teaches about how options behave.
- Control risk: spread positions, size trades sensibly, and test what happens if your inputs are wrong. (The book develops this later, e.g. Chapter 13 "Risk Considerations".)

## Common confusions
- A model value is not "the right price". It is the value *given the assumptions*.
- Model risk is separate from market risk: even if the market does nothing unusual, a bad input can make a position look cheap when it is not.
